Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons are compounds made of only carbon and hydrogen. They are our fuels (natural gas, gasoline) and the starting materials for plastics, medicines and dyes. This module covers their structures, how to name them, and how their shapes change their properties.

Lesson 1

Introduction to Hydrocarbons

Focus question: What are hydrocarbons and how are they used? → Compounds of only C and H, used as fuels and raw materials.

Key words

Organic compound
any compound containing carbon, except carbon oxides (CO, CO₂), carbides and carbonates.
Hydrocarbon
the simplest organic compound: contains only carbon and hydrogen. Simplest one: methane CH₄.

Vitalism was the old idea that only living things could make organic compounds using a "vital force". Wöhler disproved it by making urea in a lab.

Why does carbon form so many compounds?

  • Carbon is in group 14: 1s²2s²2p² → 4 valence electrons.
  • So it always forms 4 covalent bonds.
  • It bonds to H, O, N, S, P, halogens… and to other carbon atoms, making:
straight chainsbranched chainsrings

4 ways to show methane

Molecular formula = what atoms. Structural = how they connect. Ball-and-stick = 3-D shape. Space-filling = what it really looks like.

Carbon–carbon bonds

Saturated vs unsaturated

Saturated

only single bonds (C–C)

"full" of hydrogen: can't hold any more

Unsaturated

at least one double or triple bond

can react to add more atoms

Where the names came from: unsaturated hydrocarbons react with bromine, saturated ones don't (like a saturated solution can't dissolve more).

Fractional distillation of petroleum

Petroleum (crude oil) is a mixture of 1000+ compounds. Natural gas is mostly methane.

Fractional distillation separates petroleum into fractions by their boiling points.

  • Tower is hot at the bottom (~400 °C) and cool at the top.
  • Small molecules (low bp) rise high. Big molecules (high bp) condense low.

Cracking breaks big molecules into smaller ones (no oxygen, with a catalyst) to make more gasoline.

Octane rating measures how well gasoline resists knocking (burning too early). Regular ≈ 87, premium ≈ 91+.

Lesson 2

Alkanes

Focus question: What are the properties of hydrocarbons with a single bond? → Alkanes are nonpolar and not very reactive.

Alkane basics

An alkane has only single bonds between carbon atoms. Names end in -ane.

CnH2n+2
heptane (n = 7): C₇H2(7)+2 = C₇H₁₆

A homologous series is a family where each member differs by one –CH₂– unit.

Condensed formula: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃, or with brackets: CH₃(CH₂)₆CH₃ for octane.

Alkyl groups (branches)

A branch is an alkane with one H removed. Change -ane → -yl.

GroupFormula

Parent chain = longest continuous chain. Branches = substituent groups.

IUPAC naming: 6 steps

  1. Find the longest chain (it may bend!). This gives the parent name.
  2. Number from the end that gives branches the lowest numbers.
  3. Name each branch (methyl, ethyl…).
  4. Same branch more than once? Use di-, tri-, tetra-.
  5. List branches in alphabetical order (ignore di-, tri-).
  6. Write it: commas between numbers, hyphens between numbers and words.

Worked example

longest chain = 8 C → octane · branches at 3, 4, 5 (lower than 4, 5, 6) · one ethyl at 4, two methyls at 3 and 5 · ethyl before methyl → 4-ethyl-3,5-dimethyloctane

Cycloalkanes

A cyclic hydrocarbon contains a ring. Ring alkanes = cycloalkanes: add cyclo-.

CnH2n  (2 fewer H than the straight chain)

Line structure: each corner is a C; H atoms are not shown.

Naming cycloalkanes

  • The ring is always the parent, no need to find the longest chain.
  • Start numbering at a C with a branch, then go around to give the lowest set of numbers.
  • Only one branch? No number needed: methylcyclopentane.

Properties of alkanes

Nonpolar

C–C and C–H bonds are nonpolar → no hydrogen bonds.

Methane (16 amu) boils at −162 °C; water (18 amu) at 100 °C!

Don't mix with water

"Like dissolves like": alkanes dissolve in nonpolar solvents, not in water.

Used as solvents for paints and inks.

Low reactivity

No charges to attract other reactants, and C–C and C–H bonds are strong.

Lesson 3

Alkenes & Alkynes

Focus question: Why do some hydrocarbons form double and triple bonds? → Carbon can share 2 or 3 pairs of electrons with another carbon. These unsaturated hydrocarbons are more reactive.

The three families side by side

AlkanessaturatedCnH2n+2single bonds C–Cends in -aneethane C₂H₆
AlkenesunsaturatedCnH2nat least one double bond C=Cends in -eneethene C₂H₄ (ethylene)
AlkynesunsaturatedCnH2n−2at least one triple bond C≡Cends in -yneethyne C₂H₂ (acetylene)

There is no 1-carbon alkene or alkyne: you need 2 carbons to make a C=C or C≡C bond.

Naming alkenes & alkynes

  1. The parent chain is the longest chain that contains the double (or triple) bond.
  2. Number from the end closest to the double bond. The double bond decides the numbering, not the branches!
  3. Give the lower number of the two carbons in the double bond: 1-butene, 2-butene.
  4. Two double bonds? Use -diene: 2,4-heptadiene.

Numbering the double bond

"3-butene" is wrong: it is the same as 1-butene counted from the other end.

Worked example

chain with the C=C has 7 C → heptene · number from the left so C=C is at 2 · methyls at 4 and 6 → 4,6-dimethyl-2-heptene

Properties & uses of alkenes

  • Nonpolar, low solubility in water, low mp and bp.
  • More reactive than alkanes: the double bond has high electron density, a good target for reactions.
  • Ethene is a plant hormone that ripens fruit, and is used to make polyethylene plastic (bags, milk jugs).

Alkynes: the most reactive

  • Triple bond = even more electron density → more reactive than alkenes.
  • Ethyne (acetylene) burns with oxygen at about 3000 °C, hot enough for welding and cutting metal.

Reactivity: alkanes < alkenes < alkynes

Lesson 4

Hydrocarbon Isomers

Focus question: In how many ways can a set of C and H atoms be arranged? → Many! Same formula, different structure = isomers.

Isomer family tree

Structural isomers: C₅H₁₂

Isomers have the same molecular formula but different structures. Structural isomers: atoms are bonded in a different order, so they have different properties.

More branching → more compact → lower boiling point. Cyclopentane (C₅H₁₀) is not an isomer of pentane: different formula! More carbons = more isomers: C₂₀H₄₂ has over 300,000.

Geometric isomers (cis / trans)

Stereoisomers: atoms bonded in the same order but arranged differently in space. A single bond can rotate, but a double bond cannot, so groups get locked on one side.

cis = same side (Latin "on this side"). trans = across. They have different mp and bp, and can act very differently as drugs.

Optical isomers & chirality

  • Asymmetric carbon: a carbon with 4 different atoms or groups attached.
  • It can be arranged in 2 mirror-image ways that can't be stacked on each other, like your left and right hands.
  • Chirality: existing in a right-handed (d-) and left-handed (l-) form.
  • Optical isomers have the same physical and chemical properties, except in reactions where chirality matters (enzymes, drugs).

Optical rotation

Optical isomers rotate polarized light:

d-isomer

rotates light to the right ↻

dextro = right

l-isomer

rotates light to the left ↺

levo = left

Living things are picky: the body uses only l-amino acids. l-menthol tastes minty and cool; d-menthol doesn't.

Lesson 5

Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Focus question: What do mothballs, wintergreen and aspirin have in common? → They all contain a benzene ring.

The structure of benzene, C₆H₆

The puzzle

C₆H₆ has very few H, so it should be very reactive. But benzene is unreactive!

Kekulé (1865)

A hexagon with alternating single and double bonds. Explained the shape, but not the low reactivity.

Modern model

The electrons are delocalized: shared evenly by all 6 carbons. Drawn as a circle. This makes benzene very stable.

Aromatic vs aliphatic

Aromatic

contains a benzene ring

many smelled nice (spices, fruits)

Aliphatic

no benzene ring: alkanes, alkenes, alkynes

Greek aleiphatos = fat

Fused rings share sides: naphthalene (2 rings, mothballs), anthracene (3 rings, dyes).

Naming substituted benzenes

  • Named like cycloalkanes: branch names + benzene.
  • Number the ring to give the lowest numbers.
  • Alphabetical order for different branches.
methylbenzene (toluene)ethylbenzene1,4-dimethylbenzene (para-xylene)

Worked example

two propyl groups · numbering gives 1,3 (lower than 1,5) · two of them → di → 1,3-dipropylbenzene

⚠ Carcinogens

Benzene, toluene and xylene were once common solvents, but some aromatic compounds harm health (breathing, liver, nerves), and some are carcinogens (cause cancer). Benzopyrene in soot, cigarette smoke and car exhaust was the first carcinogen found. Use good ventilation and respirators.
