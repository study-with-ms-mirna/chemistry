The Periodic Table

The periodic table is a map of the elements. An element’s position tells you its valence electrons, its family and how it behaves. This chapter covers how the table was built, how elements are classified, and the periodic trends.

Section 1

Development of the Modern Periodic Table

Focus question: How are elements organized in the periodic table? → By increasing atomic number, so elements with similar properties fall in the same column.

Who built the table?

Lavoisiergrouped the 33 known elements into 4 categories
Newlandsby atomic mass; law of octaves: properties repeat every 8th element
Meyer & Mendeleevby atomic mass. Mendeleev left gaps and predicted undiscovered elements
Moseleyarranged by atomic number → the modern table

Key words

Perioda horizontal row. There are 7 periods
Group (family)a vertical column. There are 18 groups. Same group = similar properties
Representative elementsgroups 1, 2 and 13–18

Section 2

Classification of the Elements

Focus question: Why do elements in the same group have similar properties? → They have the same number of valence electrons.

Valence electrons & position

  • Period number = the energy level (n) of the valence electrons.
  • Groups 1 and 2: group number = number of valence electrons.
  • Groups 13–18: group number − 10 = valence electrons (e.g. group 16 → 6).
  • Noble gases have a full outer level: ns2 np6 (8 valence electrons; He has 2).

Group 1 has the same ending

Locating an element from its atomic number
Where is 16S in the periodic table?
  1. Write the configuration: 16S = 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p4
  2. Highest energy level = 3 ⇒ period 3
  3. Last sublevel is p ⇒ p-block (groups 13–18)
  4. Valence electrons = 3s2 + 3p4 = 6 ⇒ 6 + 10 = group 16
Sulfur: period 3, group 16, p-block
Section 3

Periodic Trends

Focus question: How can you use the periodic table to predict an element’s properties? → Properties change in a regular pattern across periods and down groups.

Why the trends happen

Across a period →more protons (stronger pull) but the same energy level ⇒ electrons are pulled closer.
Down a group ↓more energy levels, and inner electrons shield the outer ones ⇒ electrons are farther from the nucleus.

1 · Atomic radius (atom size)

  • Across a period → radius decreases.
  • Down a group ↓ radius increases.
  • Largest atoms: bottom left (Cs, Fr). Smallest: top right.

Group 1: 3Li < 11Na < 19K < 37Rb (largest).   Group 17: 9F is the smallest.

2 · Ionic radius (ion size)

Positive ions are SMALLERthe atom loses electrons (often its whole outer level), so the rest are pulled in closer. Metals form positive ions.
Negative ions are BIGGERthe atom gains electrons, which repel each other and spread out. Nonmetals form negative ions.

Same trend as atoms: ions get bigger down a group. Across a period, positive ions shrink, then the negative ions (from group 15) are larger and shrink again.

3 · Ionization energy

Ionization energythe energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom
  • Across a period → increases (electrons held more tightly).
  • Down a group ↓ decreases (outer electron is farther away).
  • Highest: noble gases (He the highest). Lowest: alkali metals.
  • Group 1: 3Li has the highest ionization energy, 55Cs the lowest.
  • Compounds (KF, KCl, KBr, KI): compare only the part that changes ⇒ F is highest, so KF.

Removing more electrons: each one needs more energy. There is a huge jump once all the valence electrons are gone. Sodium (1 valence e⁻) jumps at the 2nd ionization energy. The 2nd is always higher because the electron is removed from a positive ion (higher positive charge density).

The octet rule

Atoms gain, lose or share electrons to get a full set of 8 valence electrons.

  • Metals (low ionization energy) lose electrons → positive ions: 11Na → Na+
  • Nonmetals gain electrons → negative ions: 17Cl → Cl−
  • Valence +1, +2, +3 ⇒ metal. Noble gases: valence 0, no ionic bonds.
  • Electrons in an ion = atomic number − charge: K+ = 18, Mg2+ = 10. Cl− has the same arrangement as Ar.

4 · Electronegativity

Electronegativitythe ability of an atom to attract electrons in a chemical bond
  • Increases across →, decreases down ↓.
  • 9F is the most electronegative (3.98); halogens are the most electronegative group.
  • Noble gases have no values: they rarely form compounds.
  • Watch the distractors: down a group, atomic mass increases; electron affinity generally follows electronegativity (higher across, lower down).
