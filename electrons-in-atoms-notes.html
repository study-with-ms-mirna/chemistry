Electrons in Atoms

Atoms give off light when their electrons jump between energy levels. This chapter goes from light, to Bohr’s atom, to the modern model, and ends with writing electron configurations — the part SAAT asks about most.

Section 1

Light and Quantized Energy

Focus question: What is light made of? → Light is a wave that also behaves like a stream of particles (photons).

Key words

EM radiationenergy that travels as a wave through space: light, microwaves, X-rays, radio… Sound is NOT an EM wave (it needs a medium).
Wavelength λdistance from crest to crest. Unit: m (1 nm = 10−9 m)
Frequency νnumber of waves per second. Unit: Hz = s−1
Photona particle of light with energy but no mass (Einstein)
Quantumthe minimum amount of energy an atom can gain or lose (Planck)

The speed of light

c = λν

c = 3 × 108 m/s for all EM waves

  • λ and ν are inversely related: one goes up, the other goes down.
  • Higher ν ⇒ shorter λ ⇒ more energy.

The electromagnetic spectrum

Low → high energy: Radio · Microwave · Infrared · Visible · UV · X-rays · Gamma

Memory trick: Raging Martians Invaded Venus Using X-ray Guns.  ·  Gamma rays = high-energy photons (not particles with charge).

Energy of a photon

E = hν

h = Planck’s constant = 6.625 × 10−34 J·s

  • E is directly proportional to frequency: double ν ⇒ double E.
  • Light has a dual nature: wave and particle.

The photoelectric effect

  • Electrons are ejected only when high-frequency light (at or above the threshold frequency ν0) hits the metal.
  • Work function = hν0 (energy needed to free one electron).
  • Photon energy = hν0 ⇒ electron is ejected but has no kinetic energy.
  • Einstein explained it: light is made of photons.

Atomic emission spectra

  • Excited atoms give off light of certain frequencies only = atomic emission spectrum (lines).
  • Every element’s lines are unique, like a fingerprint.
  • An element absorbs the same frequencies it emits (absorption spectrum = dark lines).

How to calculate

① Frequency from wavelength
An EM wave has λ = 2 × 10−8 m. Find ν.
  1. Write the law and solve for ν:   c = λν ⇒ ν = cλ
  2. Substitute:   ν = 3 × 1082 × 10−8
  3. Divide the numbers: 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5
  4. Divide the powers (subtract them): 108 ÷ 10−8 = 108 − (−8) = 1016
ν = 1.5 × 1016 Hz
② Energy of a photon
A photon has ν = 2 × 1015 Hz. Find E. (h = 6.625 × 10−34 J·s)
  1. Write the law:   E = hν
  2. Substitute:   E = (6.625 × 10−34) × (2 × 1015)
  3. Multiply the numbers: 6.625 × 2 = 13.25
  4. Multiply the powers (add them): 10−34 × 1015 = 10−34 + 15 = 10−19
E = 13.25 × 10−19 J
λ given in nm? Change it to metres first: 1 nm → 1 × 10−9 m.
Section 2

Quantum Theory and the Atom

Focus question: Why does every element produce a unique emission spectrum? → Each element has its own energy levels; electrons dropping between them give off only certain energies.

Bohr’s model of the hydrogen atom

  • The electron moves only in allowed energy levels, numbered by n.
  • Ground state = lowest energy (n = 1). Gains energy ⇒ excited state.
  • Electron moves up (e.g. n = 1 → 2) ⇒ atom absorbs energy.
  • Electron moves down (e.g. n = 2 → 1) ⇒ atom emits energy as a photon ⇒ this makes the emission spectrum.
  • Bohr’s model worked for hydrogen only.

Energy levels are like a ladder

Bigger drop = more energy = higher frequency. E6 → E2 gives a higher frequency than E6 → E3 or E3 → E2. (E2 → E7 goes up, so it absorbs.)

Hydrogen’s three series

Lymandrops to n = 1 → ultraviolet
Balmerdrops to n = 2 → visible (e.g. n = 4 → 2)
Paschendrops to n = 3 → infrared (e.g. n = 4 → 3)
Memory hook: L B P → 1, 2, 3 → UV, Visible, IR

How to calculate ③ Energy of the emitted photon

Energy between two levels (in eV)
An electron drops from level B (−3.4 eV) to level A (−13.6 eV). Find the energy of the emitted photon.
  1. Write the law:   ΔE = Ehigh − Elow   (the higher level has the less negative value)
  2. Substitute:   ΔE = (−3.4) − (−13.6)
  3. Minus a minus = plus:   −3.4 + 13.6
ΔE = 10.2 eV (emitted, because the electron moved down)

The quantum mechanical model

de Brogliemoving particles, electrons too, behave like waves.
λ = hmν
m = mass (kg), ν = velocity (m/s)
Heisenberguncertainty principle: it is impossible to know an electron’s position and velocity at the same time.
Quantum mechanicsstudying matter using its wave properties ⇒ the quantum mechanical model (Schrödinger). Works for all atoms.
Electron cloudan instant picture of the electron’s movement around the nucleus: a 3-D region of probable location
Big objectsa moving car has a very small wavelength (huge mass), so we can’t see its wave nature
④ de Broglie wavelength
An electron (m = 9.11 × 10−31 kg) moves at 391 m/s. Find λ. (h = 6.625 × 10−34 J·s)
  1. Write the law:   λ = hmν
  2. Work out the bottom first: 9.11 × 10−31 × 391 = 3.56 × 10−28
  3. Divide:   6.625 × 10−343.56 × 10−28   → 6.625 ÷ 3.56 = 1.86;   10−34 − (−28) = 10−6
λ = 1.86 × 10−6 m ≈ 1.9 × 10−6 m

Levels, sublevels and orbitals

energy level n ⊃ sublevels s p d f ⊃ orbitals (max 2 e⁻ each)
  • Principal quantum number n = 1, 2, 3, … (whole numbers, never 0). It decides the energy and size of the level.
  • s = sphere (1s, 2s …).  p = dumbbell.
  • px, py, pz have the same energy and size and are at 90° to each other.

⑤ Orbitals and electrons in a level
How many orbitals and electrons can energy level n = 4 hold?
  1. Orbitals = n² = 4² = 16
  2. Electrons = 2n² = 2 × 16 = 32 (2 electrons in each orbital)
n = 4 → 16 orbitals, 32 electrons
Section 3

Electron Configuration

Focus question: How are electrons arranged in atoms? → In the lowest-energy way, following three rules.

Key words

Electron configurationthe arrangement of electrons in an atom
Ground statethe lowest-energy, most stable arrangement
Valence electronselectrons in the outermost energy level. They decide the chemical properties
Electron-dot structuresymbol + dots for the valence electrons

The three rules

1 · Aufbau principleeach electron goes into the lowest-energy orbital available
2 · Pauli exclusion principlemax 2 electrons per orbital, with opposite spins ↑↓
3 · Hund’s ruleequal-energy orbitals: one each, same spin, before pairing

Rule 1 · Aufbau: the filling order

  • Write the sublevels in rows, then follow the arrows top-right → bottom-left: arrow 1, 2, 3…
  • Each sublevel holds:
s = 2p = 6d = 10f = 14
Traps!
· 4s is lower than 3d, so 4s fills first
· 3d is higher than 4s, 3s and 2p
· 5p is higher than 4d

The order:

1s→2s→2p→3s→3p→4s→3d→4p→5s→4d→5p→6s→4f→5d→6p→7s→5f→6d→7p

Rule 2 · Pauli: arrows in boxes

box = orbital  ·  ↑ / ↓ = electron spins

Look at 7N: three single electrons in 2p (Hund). Pairing starts at 8O.

Writing a configuration step by step: 22Ti

  1. Count electrons = atomic number ⇒ 22Ti has 22 electrons.
  2. Fill in Aufbau order (s = 2, p = 6, d = 10) until you reach 22. The last sublevel takes only what is left:
    1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2 3d2
  3. Check: add the superscripts ⇒ 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 2 = 22 ✓
  4. Shorten with the noble gas before it (18Ar = first 18 electrons): [Ar] 4s2 3d2

Write 4s before 3d (filling order).  ·  Backwards: to find the element, add all superscripts ⇒ 1s2 2s2 2p5 = 9 electrons ⇒ 9F.

Configurations of ions

  • Positive ion (+): remove electrons from the highest energy level first — for transition metals that means 4s before 3d.
  • Negative ion (−): add electrons to the next empty place.

Worked example: the copper ion
Write the configuration of 29Cu2+.
  1. Start from the atom: 29Cu = [Ar] 4s1 3d10
  2. 2+ means remove 2 electrons: first the 4s1 electron, then one 3d electron
29Cu2+ = [Ar] 3d9

Valence electrons & electron-dot structures

  • Count the electrons in the highest energy level (n) only.

7N: 1s2 2s2 2p3 ⇒ 5

8O: 1s2 2s2 2p4 ⇒ 6

17Cl: [Ne] 3s2 3p5 ⇒ 7

10Ne: 1s2 2s2 2p6 ⇒ 8 (not 10!)

  • Symbol = nucleus + inner electrons.
  • Dots go one on each side first, then pair up (like Hund). Max 8.

4Be: 2 single dots  ·  5B: 3 single dots  ·  7N: 1 pair + 3 singles
