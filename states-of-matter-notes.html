States of Matter

Why is water a solid, a liquid and a gas on Earth? It depends on two things: how much energy the particles have and how strongly they attract each other. This chapter covers gases, the forces between particles, liquids & solids, and phase changes.

Section 1

Gases

Focus question: Do all gases behave the same way? → Yes, mostly. The kinetic-molecular theory explains them all by the size, motion and energy of their particles.

Solid, liquid, gas: the particle picture

Kinetic-molecular theory (KMT)

  • Size: gas particles are tiny and far apart (mostly empty space). No significant attraction or repulsion.
  • Motion: constant, random, straight-line motion until they collide.
  • Collisions are elastic: no kinetic energy is lost.
  • Energy: KE = ½mv² (mass and velocity).
  • Temperature = a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles.

What KMT explains

Low densityfew particles in a large volume (Cl₂ gas is ~6700× less dense than gold)
Compression & expansionlots of empty space ⇒ a gas can be squeezed or expand to fill any container
Diffusion & effusionparticles move freely, so gases spread out and mix

Diffusion vs effusion

Diffusionmovement of one material through another, from high to low concentration (e.g. smelling food in another room)
Effusiona gas escaping through a tiny opening (e.g. a punctured tyre)

Graham’s law of effusion

The rate of effusion (or diffusion) is inversely proportional to the square root of the molar mass. Lighter gases move faster.

RateARateB = √molar massBmolar massA
Example
Ratio of diffusion rates: NH₃ (17.0 g/mol) and HCl (36.5 g/mol)?

The lightest gas (H₂) is always the fastest.

Gas pressure

Pressure = forcearea
  • Gas particles exert pressure when they collide with the walls of their container.
  • Atmospheric (air) pressure is lower at higher altitude: fewer particles above, so fewer collisions.
  • SI unit: the pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N/m².
  • Torricelli invented the barometer: at sea level, air supports a 760 mm column of mercury.

Units of pressure

1 atm = 760 mmHg = 760 torr = 101.3 kPa

Measuring pressure

Dalton’s law of partial pressures

The total pressure of a gas mixture = the sum of the partial pressures of the gases in it.

Ptotal = P1 + P2 + P3 + …
Example
A mixture of O₂, CO₂ and N₂ has Ptotal = 0.97 atm. PCO₂ = 0.70 atm and PN₂ = 0.12 atm. Find PO₂.
  1. PO₂ = Ptotal − PCO₂ − PN₂
  2. = 0.97 − 0.70 − 0.12
PO₂ = 0.15 atm
Depends onnumber of moles, container volume and temperature. NOT on the type of gas: 1 mol of any gas gives the same partial pressure.

Section 2

Forces of Attraction

Focus question: What forces exist between molecules? → Weak intermolecular forces: dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonds.

Intramolecular vs intermolecular

Intramolecular (inside)hold particles together: ionic (NaCl), covalent (H₂), metallic (Fe) bonds
Intermolecular (between)always weaker than intramolecular forces. They decide whether a substance is a solid, liquid or gas

⚠ An ionic bond is NOT an intermolecular force.

The three intermolecular forces

1 · Dispersion (London) forces

  • Weak forces from temporary shifts in electron clouds (temporary dipoles).
  • Exist between all particles. The only force between nonpolar molecules: O₂, N₂, CH₄, I₂.
  • More electrons ⇒ stronger dispersion forces (larger molecules).

2 · Dipole–dipole forces

  • Between polar molecules, which have permanent dipoles.
  • The δ+ end of one molecule attracts the δ− end of the next (e.g. HCl).
  • Stronger than dispersion forces in small molecules.

3 · Hydrogen bonds

  • A special, strong dipole–dipole attraction.
  • Needs H bonded to F, O or N (small, very electronegative atoms).
  • Examples: H₂O, NH₃, HF. Not CH₄, HCl or Cl₂.

Why hydrogen bonds matter: similar masses, very different boiling points

CompoundMolar massStrongest forceBoiling pointAt room temperature
Water H₂O18 g/molhydrogen bonds (O–H, very polar)100 °Cliquid
Ammonia NH₃17 g/molhydrogen bonds (weaker: N less electronegative)−33.3 °Cgas
Methane CH₄16 g/moldispersion only (nonpolar)−161.5 °Cgas

Water is a liquid at room temperature because of hydrogen bonds.

Section 3

Liquids and Solids

Focus question: What are the properties of liquids and solids? → KMT still applies, but now the attractions between particles matter.

Liquids

  • Take the shape of the container, but have a fixed volume.
  • Much denser than gases; almost incompressible (particles already close).
  • Fluids: liquids and gases can flow and diffuse. Liquids diffuse more slowly than gases.

Viscosity

Viscosityresistance of a liquid to flow (honey: high; water: low)
FactorViscosity
stronger intermolecular forces↑ higher (glycerol: many H-bonds)
larger, longer molecules↑ higher (oils)
higher temperature↓ lower (warm oil flows easily)

Surface tension

Surface tensionthe energy needed to increase the surface area of a liquid; a measure of the inward pull on surface particles
  • Stronger attractions ⇒ higher surface tension. Water is high (hydrogen bonds), so a spider can walk on it and drops are round.
  • Surfactants (soaps, detergents) lower surface tension by breaking hydrogen bonds.

Cohesion, adhesion & capillary action

Cohesionattraction between identical molecules (water–water)
Adhesionattraction between different substances (water–glass)
Capillary actiona liquid rises up a narrow tube because adhesion pulls it up (paper towels soak up water)

Solids

  • Definite shape and volume: strong attractions hold particles in fixed positions; they only vibrate.
  • Usually denser than their liquids (solid sinks in its liquid).
  • Exception: ice floats. Hydrogen bonds hold water molecules in an open pattern, so ice is less dense than liquid water. Water expands when it freezes.

Why ice floats

Crystalline vs amorphous solids

Crystalline solidatoms, ions or molecules in an orderly, repeating pattern (crystal lattice). The unit cell is the smallest repeating unit
Amorphous solidparticles not in a regular pattern. Forms when a melt cools too quickly. Glass, rubber, many plastics

Five types of crystalline solids

TypeParticlesPropertiesExamples
Atomicatoms (noble gases)soft, very low melting points, poor conductorsgroup 18
Molecularmolecules (dispersion, dipole, H-bonds)fairly soft, low melting points, poor conductorsI₂, H₂O (ice), CO₂, sugar

Allotropes = different forms of the same element in the same state. Carbon: diamond, graphite, buckminsterfullerene.

Section 4

Phase Changes

Focus question: What causes a substance to change phases? → Adding or removing energy.

The six phase changes

endothermic: energy ABSORBED (ΔH +)exothermic: energy RELEASED (ΔH −)
  • Adding energy breaks attractions → particles move more freely.
  • Each change has a reverse with the opposite energy change.
  • Melting point = freezing point for the same substance.

Each phase change

ChangeFrom → toEnergyRemember
Meltingsolid → liquidabsorbedmelting point: solid and liquid coexist (amorphous solids have none)
Vaporizationliquid → gasabsorbedevaporation = at the surface only, below the boiling point
Sublimationsolid → gasabsorbeddry ice (CO₂), iodine I₂(s) → I₂(g), mothballs
Freezingliquid → solidreleasedreverse of melting; freezing point = melting point
Condensationgas → liquidreleasedreverse of vaporization: dew, drops on a cold glass
Depositiongas → solidreleasedreverse of sublimation: frost, snowflakes. H₂O(g) → H₂O(s) + heat (ΔH −)

Vapor pressure & boiling point

Vapor pressurethe pressure exerted by a vapor over its liquid in a closed container; it rises with temperature
Boiling pointthe temperature at which vapor pressure = external (atmospheric) pressure

Water boils at 100 °C at 101.3 kPa (1 atm). At high altitude the air pressure is lower, so water boils below 100 °C.

Phase diagrams

A phase diagram is a graph of pressure vs temperature showing which phase exists under each set of conditions.

Linestwo phases exist together (e.g. on the blue line, liquid and vapor coexist)
Triple pointthe only point where all three phases coexist. Water: 0.01 °C, 0.006 atm
Critical pointabove this temperature and pressure the substance can no longer exist as a liquid. Water: 374 °C, 217.75 atm
CO₂at 1 atm it goes straight from solid to gas, so dry ice sublimes (−78 °C)

Water’s solid–liquid line leans to the left because ice is less dense than liquid water. Carbon’s diagram shows two solids: graphite (normal conditions) and diamond (very high pressure).
