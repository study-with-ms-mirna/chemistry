Substituted Hydrocarbons

Swap a hydrogen in a hydrocarbon for another atom or group, a functional group, and you get a new family of compounds with new properties. This chapter covers the families, how to name them, their reactions, and polymers.

Alkyl Halides & Aryl Halides

Focus question: What happens when a halogen replaces a hydrogen? → The compound gets a higher boiling point and density and becomes more reactive.

Key words

Halocarbon
any organic compound containing a halogen substituent.
Alkyl halide
halogen bonded to an aliphatic carbon (chain). e.g. chloromethane CH₃Cl
Aryl halide
halogen bonded to a benzene ring. e.g. chlorobenzene

The simplest functional group that can be bonded to a hydrocarbon is a halogen. General formula of alkyl halides: R–X.

Naming halocarbons

  • Change -ine → -o: fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-
  • Number the chain (or ring) to give the halogens the lowest numbers.
  • Two of the same? di-, tri-: 1,2-difluoropropane
  • Different halogens → alphabetical order (bromo, chloro, fluoro, iodo).
  • Halogen + alkyl branch? Treat them the same: alphabetical order, lowest numbers: 2-bromo-3-methylbutane.
  • Has a C=C or C≡C? The multiple bond gets the lowest number first, then the halogens: 5-chloro-2-pentyne.
  • Rings: a ring with a double bond starts at the C=C (C1 and C2). Benzene: number from a halogen to get the lowest set.

Worked examples

4 C → butane · Br, F, Cl at 1, 2, 3 · alphabetical: bromo, chloro, fluoro → 1-bromo-3-chloro-2-fluorobutane
Ring with C=C: C1=C2 first, then the substituents get the lowest numbers → 3-chloro-2-methylcyclopentene, 4-bromo-5-chlorocyclohexene

Boiling point rises with the halogen

pentane36 °C
1-fluoropentane63 °C
1-chloropentane108 °C
1-bromopentane130 °C
1-iodopentane155 °C

Alkyl halides have higher bp and density than their parent alkane. Going F → Cl → Br → I: more electrons → stronger temporary dipoles (dispersion forces) → bp and density increase.

Lesson 2

Alcohols, Ethers & Amines

Focus question: How does adding –OH or –NH₂ change a hydrocarbon? → It becomes polar and can hydrogen bond: higher bp, more soluble in water.

Alcohols: R–OH

An alcohol has a hydroxyl group (–OH) in place of an H.

  • Polar + hydrogen bonds → much higher bp than hydrocarbons of similar size.
  • Mix well with water: ethanol is completely miscible.
  • Methanol CH₃OH is the simplest alcohol.
  • Glycerol (1,2,3-propanetriol) has 3 –OH groups. It stops jet fuel from freezing.

Naming alcohols

  1. Take the alkane name, change -e → -ol: methane → methanol CH₃OH, ethane → ethanol CH₃CH₂OH.
  2. 3 or more C: add the number of the C holding the –OH (lowest possible).
  3. More than one –OH: keep the -e and add -diol, -triol.
  4. Branched? Find the longest chain that holds the C–OH, number from the –OH end, name the branch: 3-methyl-1-pentanol.

Ring with –OH: cyclo + alkane + -ol: cyclobutanol, cyclopentanol, cyclohexanol (no number needed). The parent alkane of butanol is butane.

Examples

"4-butanol" is wrong: count from the other end → 1-butanol. Always use the lowest number.

Ethers: R–O–R'

An ether has an oxygen bonded to two carbons.

  • No H on the O → can't hydrogen bond with each other → lower bp, more volatile than alcohols.
  • Less soluble in water than alcohols (can only accept H-bonds from water).
  • Oils and fats dissolve in ethers, not in water.
  • Diethyl ether was used as an anesthetic in surgery.
Naming ethers

Different groups → name both in alphabetical order + ether.

Same group on both sides → add di- + ether (dimethyl ether, diethyl ether).

Hydrogen bonds & boiling point

A hydrogen bond forms between an H on O (or N) of one molecule and an O (or N) of another molecule. More/stronger attractions → more energy needed to separate molecules → higher boiling point.

Alcohol + alcohol ✓

O–H has a δ+ H → it is attracted to the δ− O of the next molecule.

Ether + ether ✗

All H are on C, none on O → no hydrogen bond → weaker attraction → lower bp.

Boiling point ladder (molecules of similar size, ~60 g/mol)

butane (alkane, nonpolar)−1 °C
ethyl methyl ether (polar, no H-bond)7 °C
propylamine (N–H: weak H-bond)48 °C
propanal (polar C=O)49 °C
propanone (polar C=O)56 °C
1-propanol (O–H: H-bond)97 °C
ethanoic acid (2 H-bonds per pair)118 °C
alkane < ether < amine ≈ aldehyde < ketone < alcohol < carboxylic acid

Solubility in water also depends on hydrogen bonds, this time with water. See the solubility box at the end of Lesson 3: carboxylic acids are the most soluble.

Amines: R–NH₂

An amine has an amino group (–NH₂): nitrogen bonded to carbon. Amines come from ammonia NH₃.

  • Name: alkyl group + -amine: CH₃NH₂ methylamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂ ethylamine.
  • Amines cause the smell of dead bodies. Dogs find disaster victims by smelling amines.
  • Amines have no carbonyl group (don't confuse with amides!).

Substitution reactions

A substitution reaction: one atom or group is replaced by another.

Halogenation: an H is replaced by a halogen (F, Cl, Br; not I).

CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl
C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl

propane + Br₂ → bromopropane + HBr

Then the halogen can be swapped:

C₂H₅Cl + NaOH → C₂H₅OH + NaCl  (alcohol)
R–X + NH₃ → R–NH₂ + HX  (amine)
Lesson 3

Carbonyl Compounds

Focus question: How do hydroxyl and carbonyl groups compare? → Both are polar, but the carbonyl group C=O has no H on O, so it can't H-bond to itself.

The carbonyl family

A carbonyl group = a C atom double-bonded to an O (C=O). Five families contain it: aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, esters, amides. No carbonyl: alcohols, ethers, amines.

AldehydeC=O at the endending -al
KetoneC=O in the middleending -one
Carboxylic acid–COOHending -oic acid

Aldehydes

  • Group –CHO at the end of the chain → no number needed: CH₃CH₂CHO = propanal.
  • Polar, but no H-bonds between molecules → bp and solubility lower than alcohols.
  • Formaldehyde (methanal, formalin) preserves biological samples for years.
  • Benzaldehyde (almond smell) → perfumes and flavorings.

Ketones

  • C=O inside the chain, bonded to two carbons.
  • General formula R–CO–R'. Change -e → -one + number: CH₃CH₂COCH₃ = 2-butanone.
  • Simplest and most common: propanone (acetone).
  • Never "1-…one": a C=O at C1 would be an aldehyde.
  • Polar → good solvents for polar compounds. Acetone = nail polish remover.

Carboxylic acids

Carboxyl group –COOH = carbonyl + hydroxyl on the same C.

  • General formula R–COOH. The most polar family in this chapter; weak acids.
  • Two –COOH = dicarboxylic acid (oxalic, adipic). Salicylic acid is also a carboxylic acid.
  • Same –COOH group → same chemical properties (C₃H₇COOH and CH₃COOH react alike).
CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O

CH₃COONa = sodium acetate (a salt).

vinegar + sodium bicarbonate → CO₂ gas (bubbles)

Ethanoic acid (acetic acid) = vinegar. Methanoic acid (formic acid) = what ants produce to defend themselves.

Esters & amides (from acids)

Ester RCOOR'

H of –COOH replaced by an alkyl group.

Name: alkyl + acid -ic → -ate

Fruity smell → found in perfumes, fruits and roses. Ethyl butanoate = pineapple; methyl hexanoate = strawberries.

Amide R–CO–NHR'

–OH of –COOH replaced by N.

Name: -e → -amide: CH₃CH₂CONH₂ = propanamide

Urea NH₂CONH₂ (carbamide) is an amide.

Examples

Condensation reaction

Two small organic molecules join into a bigger one and lose a small molecule (usually water).

carboxylic acid + alcohol → ester + water    RCOOH + R'OH → RCOOR' + H₂O

This is called esterification. Example: ethanol + acetic acid → ethyl ethanoate (an ester) + water.

Solubility in water: count the hydrogen bonds!

A molecule dissolves in water when it can hydrogen bond with water molecules. It can do this in two ways:

Give an H-bond

it has an H on O or N (O–H, N–H) that is attracted to the O of water

Receive an H-bond

it has an O or N that attracts the H of water

More H-bonds with water → more soluble.

Ethanoic acid in water: the winner

The –COOH group gives one H-bond (from its O–H) and receives H-bonds on two oxygens (C=O and O–H), so it makes the most H-bonds with water.

Other Reactions of Organic Compounds

Focus question: What reactions are typical of organic compounds? → 5 types: substitution, elimination, addition, oxidation–reduction, condensation.

Elimination: make a double bond

Atoms are removed from two neighbouring C → a new double bond forms. A small molecule (H₂, HX, H₂O) leaves.

Dehydrogenation
lose H₂
CH₃–CH₃ → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂
From alkyl halide
lose HX
R–CH₂–CH₂–X → R–CH=CH₂ + HX
Dehydration
lose H₂O (alcohol → alkene)
CH₃–CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O

alkane → alkene, alkyl halide → alkene, alcohol → alkene are all elimination.

Oxidation–reduction

Oxidation

Reduction

Oxidizing agents: K₂Cr₂O₇, KMnO₄, PCC. Reducing agents: NaBH₄, LiAlH₄.

Oxidation & reduction of alcohols

  • 1° alcohol → aldehyde → (strong) carboxylic acid: 1-propanol → propanal → propanoic acid; ethanol → ethanal → ethanoic acid
  • 2° alcohol → ketone, then stops: 2-propanol → 2-propanone
  • 3° alcohol → no oxidation
  • Reduction goes backwards: aldehyde → 1° alcohol; ketone → 2° alcohol (propanone → 2-propanol)

Lesson 5

Polymers

Focus question: Why are polymers so important? → They are cheap, easy to shape, durable and have a huge range of properties.

Key words

Polymer
a very large molecule made of many repeating units.
Monomer
the small molecule a polymer is made from.
Structural unit
the repeating group in the polymer, shown in brackets with n.
Polymerization
the reaction that bonds monomers together (usually needs a catalyst).
n CH₂=CH₂ → –[ CH₂–CH₂ ]ₙ–   polyethylene

Properties of polymers

  • Most do NOT dissolve in water.
  • Chemically unreactive (don't rust or decay).
  • Poor conductors of electricity → insulation.
  • Easy to shape → many industrial uses.

Polyethylene: waxy, insoluble in water, unreactive, electrical insulator.
